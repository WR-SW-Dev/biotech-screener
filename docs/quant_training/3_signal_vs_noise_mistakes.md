# Signal vs Noise — 10 Common Mistakes in Biotech Event Analysis

### Signal vs Noise Mistakes in Biotech Event Analysis

#### 1. Small-Sample IC Inflation
**Example:** A biotech company reports a significant increase in its intellectual property portfolio after acquiring another small firm.
**Why It's Noise:** The acquisition might have been purely financial, with no real impact on the core technology or innovation.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Analyze the quality and relevance of the acquired IP rather than just counting it.

#### 2. Survivorship Bias
**Example:** A study shows a high survival rate for patients using a new drug in clinical trials.
**Why It's Noise:** Only patients who survived to report their outcomes are included, ignoring those who dropped out or died before completion.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Use Kaplan-Meier curves and include all patient data, including dropouts.

#### 3. Price Action as POS Proxy
**Example:** A biotech stock sees a sharp rise after positive news about a new drug candidate.
**Why It's Noise:** The price action may be driven by speculation rather than genuine clinical progress.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Focus on underlying clinical data, regulatory milestones, and analyst consensus.

#### 4. Analyst Consensus
**Example:** A biotech company sees its stock price rise after a strong analyst consensus report.
**Why It's Noise:** Analysts may be influenced by factors other than the actual drug performance.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Cross-reference analyst opinions with independent market research and clinical data.

#### 5. Management Guidance Bias
**Example:** A biotech company announces that its lead drug will enter Phase III trials in Q4.
**Why It's Noise:** The guidance may be optimistic or based on incomplete data.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Look for historical performance, competitive landscape, and peer comparisons.

#### 6. Endpoint Substitution
**Example:** A biotech company reports positive results from a Phase II trial using a different endpoint than planned in the original protocol.
**Why It's Noise:** The new endpoint may not be as meaningful or comparable to the original one.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Ensure that any changes in endpoints are justified and have been validated by peer review.

#### 7. Enrollment Rate
**Example:** A biotech company sees a dramatic increase in enrollment for its Phase III trial.
**Why It's Noise:** The increase may be due to marketing efforts rather than genuine interest or efficacy.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Compare enrollment rates with historical data and industry benchmarks, and consider the impact of marketing campaigns.

#### 8. Regulatory Approval Timing
**Example:** A biotech company sees its stock price rise after regulatory approval is announced.
**Why It's Noise:** The approval may be based on interim data or not yet fully validated.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Focus on long-term outcomes, patient safety, and market penetration.

#### 9. Peer Comparison Bias
**Example:** A biotech company sees its stock price rise after a peer announces a positive clinical trial result.
**Why It's Noise:** The comparison may be based on incomplete or biased data.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Use multiple sources of data, including internal company reports and independent market research.

#### 10. Overreliance on Clinical Trials
**Example:** A biotech company sees its stock price rise after a positive Phase II clinical trial result.
**Why It's Noise:** The results may not be predictive of long-term success or regulatory approval.
**Disciplined Alternative:** Consider all available data, including preclinical studies, market trends, and competitive landscape.
